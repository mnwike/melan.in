Sea moss and moringa for skin and hair

Sea moss and moringa are promoted for glowing skin and hair growth across the Caribbean, Africa and India. What they contain, what is proven, and the risks.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Neither sea moss nor moringa has been shown in human trials to improve skin or grow hair.
  • Both are nutritious foods. Moringa leaves are rich in vitamins and iron.
  • Sea moss can contain a lot of iodine and sometimes heavy metals. Too much can upset the thyroid.

Limited evidence

Sea moss

Sea moss, or Irish moss, is a red seaweed long used in Caribbean drinks and in Ireland. It contains fibre, minerals and iodine. Claims that it supplies '92 of the body's 102 minerals' have no scientific basis. As a gel on the skin it is a mild humectant. Eaten in large amounts it can deliver more iodine than is safe, and seaweeds can accumulate arsenic, lead and mercury from the water they grow in.

Limited evidence

Moringa

Moringa is a tree native to South Asia and widely grown in Africa. Its leaves are a good source of protein, vitamin A, vitamin C and iron, and are valuable where diets are short of those. Laboratory studies show antioxidant effects. There are no good human trials for skin or hair. Moringa seed oil, sometimes called ben oil, is a pleasant, stable skin and hair oil.

Mixed evidence

Sensible use

  • Treat both as foods, not medicines.
  • Avoid sea moss supplements if you have a thyroid condition or are pregnant, unless your doctor agrees.
  • Buy from suppliers that test for heavy metals.
  • Moringa root and bark should be avoided in pregnancy.
  • If hair is thinning or skin is dull, ask for iron, vitamin D and thyroid checks.

When to see a doctor

Palpitations, weight change, neck swelling or tremor after taking sea moss, or hair loss that continues.

Common questions

Is sea moss good for your skin?

There is no trial evidence. It is a food with iodine, and too much can affect the thyroid.
