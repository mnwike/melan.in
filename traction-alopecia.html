Thinning edges and traction alopecia

Hair loss along the hairline from tight braids, weaves and ponytails. Which styles carry the most risk, the early signs, and how to reverse it.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Traction alopecia is hair loss from repeated pulling on the root.
  • It is preventable, and reversible if caught early. Left too long, the loss becomes permanent.
  • If a style hurts, it is too tight.

Strong evidence

What the research found

Dermatologists at Johns Hopkins reviewed 19 studies and confirmed a strong link between scalp-pulling styles and hair loss. An estimated one in three African-American women is affected, which makes it the most common form of hair loss in that group.

  • Highest risk: braids, locs, weaves and extensions, especially on chemically straightened hair.
  • Moderate risk: the same styles on natural hair, plus heat straightening and wigs.
  • Lowest risk: loose, low-tension styles and wearing the hair down.

Strong evidence

Early signs

  • Soreness or small bumps at the hairline after styling.
  • Shorter, broken hairs along the edges.
  • A hairline that is slowly moving back.

Strong evidence

What helps

  • Ask for braids to be looser, especially at the front.
  • Keep braids in for no more than two to three months, and take weaves and extensions out after six to eight weeks.
  • Rest the hair between installs.
  • Avoid combining tension with relaxers.

When to see a doctor

A widening parting, shiny smooth patches of scalp, or any hair loss that continues after you have loosened your styles.

Common questions

Can edges grow back?

Yes, if the pulling stops early. Once follicles have scarred, the loss is permanent.

Are protective styles safe?

They can be, when they are loose, not too heavy, and not left in for too long.
