African black soap

African black soap comes from West Africa and is made from plantain ash, cocoa pods and shea butter. What it does, why it can dry and sting, and how to use it.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Black soap is a traditional West African cleanser, known as ose dudu in Yoruba and alata samina in Ghana.
  • It cleans well and suits oily skin. It is strongly alkaline, so it can dry and sting.
  • It does not lighten skin or cure acne or eczema, though many people find it helps oily, spot-prone skin.

Strong evidence

What it is

Plantain skins, cocoa pods or palm leaves are burnt to ash, which supplies the alkali. The ash is cooked with palm kernel oil, coconut oil and shea butter. Real black soap is brown, soft and crumbly, not jet black and hard. Recipes vary by maker and region.

Limited evidence

What it does

It is a soap with some unsaponified shea butter left in. Small laboratory studies show antibacterial activity, and surveys of users report satisfaction for acne, dark spots and razor bumps. There are no controlled trials. Its pH is around 9 to 10, far above the skin's own, which explains the tight, squeaky feeling.

Limited evidence

Using it

  • Lather in your hands first. Do not rub the bar on your face: bits of ash can scratch.
  • Start two or three times a week.
  • Rinse well and moisturise straight away.
  • Avoid on eczema, broken skin and very dry skin.
  • Store dry. It dissolves quickly in water.
  • On hair it cleans thoroughly and can leave it stripped. Follow with conditioner.

When to see a doctor

Burning, a rash, or dryness and cracking that does not settle when you stop.

Common questions

Is African black soap good for your face?

It suits oily skin for many people, but it is alkaline and drying. Moisturise after and avoid it on dry or eczema-prone skin.
