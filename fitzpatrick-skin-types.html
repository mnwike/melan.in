Fitzpatrick skin types I to VI

The Fitzpatrick scale sorts skin into six types by how it burns and tans. What each type means, what it is used for, and why it fits darker skin poorly.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • The scale has six types, from I (always burns) to VI (never burns, deeply pigmented).
  • It was made in 1975 to set doses of ultraviolet treatment for white patients with psoriasis.
  • It measures reaction to sun. It was never a measure of skin colour or of race.

Strong evidence

Where it came from

Thomas Fitzpatrick, a Harvard dermatologist, devised the scale in 1975 to choose a safe starting dose of ultraviolet light for psoriasis treatment. He began with four types for white skin, based on a simple question: when you first go out in summer sun, do you burn, and do you tan? Types V and VI, for brown and black skin, were added later.

Strong evidence

How it is used now

  • Choosing laser and light settings, where the wrong setting burns darker skin.
  • Judging peel strength and procedure risk.
  • Describing who was included in research.
  • Sunscreen advice.
  • Skin tone options for emoji, which are based on it.

Mixed evidence

Where it falls short

  • Three of the six types divide up pale skin. The enormous range of brown and black skin is squeezed into the rest.
  • It depends on people describing their own burning and tanning. People with dark skin often say they never burn when they do: the burn shows as tightness, heat and peeling, not redness.
  • Clinicians often guess the type from appearance or ethnicity, which is not what the scale measures. A 2026 paper warns against using it as a stand-in for skin colour or race.
  • A UK review argued that labelling people IV to VI gives a false sense that skin cancer is not their concern.
  • Two people of the same type can react very differently to a laser or a peel.

Limited evidence

What might replace it

Researchers increasingly measure colour directly with a handheld meter, or use wider colour scales with ten or more tones. Others record what matters clinically: does this person's skin mark after inflammation, and do they form keloids? None has yet displaced the six types in everyday practice.

Limited evidence

Using it sensibly

Treat your type as a rough guide to sun reaction and nothing else. Before any laser, peel or light treatment, ask the practitioner how they adjust for your skin and whether they will do a test patch.

When to see a doctor

Before any laser, intense pulsed light or medium-depth peel on brown or black skin, see a practitioner who treats skin of colour regularly.

Common questions

What Fitzpatrick type is Black skin?

Usually described as V or VI, but the scale measures reaction to sun, and darker skin tones vary far more than two types can capture.

Is the Fitzpatrick scale a measure of race?

No. It was designed to predict burning and tanning, and using it as a stand-in for race is a recognised error.
