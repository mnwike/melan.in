Relaxers explained: lye, no-lye and texturisers

How hair relaxers work, the difference between lye, no-lye and thio relaxers and texturisers, what each does to hair and scalp, and how to lower the risk.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • All relaxers straighten by permanently breaking bonds inside the hair. That weakens it.
  • Lye relaxers act fast and burn the scalp more easily. No-lye relaxers sting less and dry the hair more.
  • Every type can cause burns, breakage and hair loss when misused.

Strong evidence

The main types

  • Lye: sodium hydroxide, at a very high pH of about 12.5 to 14. The oldest and strongest type, mainly used in salons.
  • No-lye: calcium hydroxide mixed with guanidine carbonate to form guanidine hydroxide. Sold in home kits. Gentler on the scalp, but it leaves mineral deposits that dry the hair.
  • Lithium or potassium hydroxide: no-mix 'no-lye' relaxers that are chemically close to lye.
  • Thio (ammonium thioglycolate): a milder chemistry at a lower pH, used for looser textures and in perms.

Mixed evidence

Texturisers and 'kiddie' relaxers

A texturiser is a relaxer left on for a shorter time to loosen the curl instead of removing it. Products sold for children use the same family of chemicals. Dermatologists advise against chemical straightening for children.

Strong evidence

What they do to hair and scalp

Relaxers work by controlled damage to the hair's protein. Hair specialists report that all types can cause scalp erosion and burning, thinning, breakage and, at worst, permanent hair loss with scarring. Common mistakes are relaxing too often and reapplying to hair that has already been relaxed.

Mixed evidence

Lowering the risk

  • Have it applied by a trained professional where you can.
  • Touch up new growth only, every eight to ten weeks at most.
  • Protect the scalp with a base cream, and never apply to a scratched or sore scalp.
  • Do not combine with tight braids or weaves, which raise the risk of hair loss.

Strong evidence

Relaxers and cancer

Two large studies link long-term, frequent relaxer use with uterine cancer. See the evidence, and its limits, in our guides below.

When to see a doctor

A scalp burn, patches of hair coming out after a relaxer, or tenderness at the crown.

Common questions

Is no-lye relaxer safer than lye?

It is gentler on the scalp but tends to dry the hair more. Both can burn and break hair.

How often should you relax your hair?

Dermatologists suggest touching up new growth no more than every eight to ten weeks.
