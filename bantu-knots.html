Bantu knots

Bantu knots are a southern African style worn as it is or taken down for curls. How to do them without tension, how long to keep them, and the history.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Bantu knots use no heat and no added hair.
  • Tight knots pull at the roots like any tight style. They should not hurt.
  • Hair must be fully dry before a knot-out, or it will frizz.

Mixed evidence

Where they come from

The style is associated with the Zulu and other Bantu-speaking peoples of southern Africa and has been worn for centuries. 'Bantu' refers to a large family of languages and the peoples who speak them. The knots are also called Zulu knots, and in the Caribbean, chiney bumps.

Limited evidence

How to do them

  • Start on stretched, slightly damp hair with a leave-in.
  • Part into sections. Larger sections give looser waves, smaller ones tighter curls.
  • Twist each section until it coils on itself, then wind it round its base.
  • Tuck the end under, or secure with a pin or small band.
  • Keep the base loose enough that the scalp is not pulled into a peak.

Limited evidence

Wearing and sleeping

  • They last up to about a week.
  • Sleep on satin. A bonnet over knots can be uncomfortable, so a satin pillowcase is easier.
  • For a knot-out, leave them overnight or until fully dry, unwind in the direction of the twist, and separate with oiled fingers.

Mixed evidence

Kindness to the hairline

  • Do not knot the fine hairs at the edges tightly.
  • Move the partings each time.
  • If knots give you a headache or small bumps, they are too tight.

When to see a doctor

Pain, bumps or pus along the hairline or partings, a widening parting, or patches where hair is not growing back. Take the style out and see a GP or dermatologist.

Common questions

Do bantu knots damage hair?

Not if they are loose at the base. Tight knots can cause traction like any tight style.
