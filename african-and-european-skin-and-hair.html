African and European skin and hair compared

A careful comparison of skin and hair in people of African and European ancestry: sun, ageing, scarring, pigment marks, hair shape and care, and shared ground.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • The two differ most in pigment and in hair shape. Almost everything else follows from those two facts.
  • Each has a matching set of strengths and vulnerabilities. Neither is 'better' skin or hair.
  • Most skincare and haircare advice was written for European skin and hair. That is a gap in research, not a fact of biology.

Strong evidence

Read this first

The studies behind this page use labels such as 'African', 'Asian' and 'Caucasian'. Those are rough social groupings, not biological types. Each group contains huge variety, the ranges overlap, and many studies measured only a few dozen people in one city. Read every figure here as an average from a sample, never as a rule about a person.

Both labels are broad. 'African ancestry' spans the greatest genetic and skin tone diversity of any continent. 'European' runs from very fair, freckled skin to olive skin that tans deeply. Many people have both ancestries.

Strong evidence

Sun

Deeply pigmented skin filters ultraviolet many times more effectively than fair skin. A person with fair European skin can burn in minutes in strong sun and has the highest rates of sun-caused skin cancer in the world. A person with dark African skin burns rarely, though it happens, and seldom gets those cancers. The cost is on the other side: in a British winter, dark skin makes very little vitamin D. UK guidance advises a supplement for people with dark skin.

Mixed evidence

Ageing

Fair skin shows sun damage early: fine lines, thread veins, rough patches. Dark skin keeps its structure longer and shows age later as uneven tone, darker circles, and gradual loss of fullness in the cheeks.

Strong evidence

Injury and inflammation

This reverses the advantage. After acne, a cut, a rash or a cosmetic procedure, fair skin goes pink and fades. Dark skin commonly lays down pigment that lasts months, and is more likely to form raised or keloid scars. That is why picking spots, harsh scrubs, and aggressive lasers and peels cost more in darker skin.

Mixed evidence

Recognising illness

Redness is the textbook sign of inflammation, and it is a sign defined on pale skin. On dark skin the same inflammation looks purple, brown or grey. Eczema, cellulitis, rashes and pressure damage are all recognised later as a result. That is a training problem. The skin is not harder to read once you know what to look for.

Mixed evidence

Hair

European hair is typically round to oval, straight to curly, and the densest on the scalp. African hair is flattened, tightly coiled, less dense and slower growing on average, and it breaks under less force. Scalp oil coats a straight hair within a day or two and barely travels down a coil.

  • So straight hair is usually washed often to remove oil. Coiled hair is washed less often and moisturised more.
  • Brushing dry suits straight hair. Coiled hair is best detangled wet with conditioner.
  • Pattern baldness is the typical European hair loss. Traction and scarring loss at the crown are the patterns to watch in African hair.
  • Close shaving rarely troubles straight beards. Curved hairs grow back into the skin, causing razor bumps.

Strong evidence

Shared ground

The same cells, the same proteins, the same conditions. Acne, eczema, psoriasis, dandruff and hair thinning occur in both. Sunscreen, moisturiser, gentle cleansing and not smoking help everyone. The differences are of degree and of presentation.

When to see a doctor

Anyone of any skin tone: a changing mole or spot, a sore that will not heal, or hair loss that is sudden, patchy or painful.

Common questions

What is the main difference between Black and white skin?

The amount and packaging of melanin. That changes how skin responds to sun and to injury. The underlying structure is the same.

Does Black skin age better?

It shows sun-related wrinkles later because melanin filters ultraviolet. It is more prone to uneven tone and dark marks.
