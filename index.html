What melanin is

Everyone has it. We carry more of it, and carry it differently.

Melanin is the pigment that colours skin, hair and eyes. It is made by cells called melanocytes, packed into tiny parcels called melanosomes, and passed to the cells around them. It is not a flaw to correct or a problem to manage. It is one of the oldest protections the human body has.

Same cells

The number of melanocytes barely differs

Deep brown skin and pale skin hold a similar count of pigment cells. Darker skin is not explained by having more of them.

Two pigments

Eumelanin and pheomelanin

Eumelanin gives brown and black tones. Pheomelanin gives red and yellow tones. Every person makes a blend of the two.

The parcels

What changes is the packaging

Tone comes from the type and amount of melanin, and from the size, number and spread of the melanosomes that hold it.

Sources: Moreiras et al., melanin transfer in the epidermis · Del Bino et al., diversity of human skin pigmentation

History and diversity

A story written by sunlight.

Where the sun is strongest, people evolved deep, eumelanin-rich skin that shields the body from ultraviolet light. As people settled where sunlight is weaker, skin lightened so the body could still make vitamin D. Anthropologists Nina Jablonski and George Chaplin traced this pattern with satellite measurements of UV across the globe.

Lighter skin arose more than once, by different genetic routes. Skin colour is a gradient shaped by geography and migration. It has never been a boundary between kinds of people, even though it has long been used to rank them. This library starts from the opposite place.

Sources: Jablonski & Chaplin, PNAS 2010 · The colours of humanity, 2017

Skin atlas

How skin gets measured, and where the rulers fall short.

We will map skin by what can be measured: tone, response to sun, oil and water balance, and the conditions that show up differently on darker skin. We start with the scales themselves.

Fitzpatrick scale

Six types, devised in 1975 to predict how skin reacts to ultraviolet light. It was never meant to describe tone, yet it became the default, and it gives the deepest tones only two of its six boxes.

Monk Skin Tone scale

Ten open-source tones from sociologist Ellis Monk, with six of them given to medium and deeper shades. Built so cameras, sensors and studies stop treating dark skin as an afterthought.

Measured colour

Instruments such as colorimeters read skin as numbers instead of categories. Researchers increasingly ask for these readings in trials, so results can be checked across the whole range of tones.

Sources: Journal of Drugs in Dermatology, Fitzpatrick and Monk compared · STAT on the Monk scale

Hair atlas

Eight curl types, measured without a single mention of race.

A study of 2,449 people in 22 countries sorted hair by its geometry alone: curve diameter, curl index, waves and twists. It found eight groups that run from straight to tightly coiled and that cut across every population.

The salon shorthand most of us know, types 1 to 4 with letters A to C, came from a stylist in 1997. We will show both, side by side, with what each can and cannot tell you about care.

Source: Loussouarn et al., 2007

Product watch

What is sold to us, what people say about it, and what the evidence says.

We read the studies and the reviews so you do not have to. Every risk is shown with the size of the study behind it and how sure anyone can be.

Risk flagged

Chemical hair relaxers

A US study followed 33,497 women for about 11 years. An estimated 1.64% of those who never used straighteners developed uterine cancer by 70, against 4.05% of frequent users.

How sure: an association in one large cohort, not proof of cause. Uterine cancer remains relatively rare. Read the summary

Risk flagged

Skin-lightening creams

Mercury is added to some lightening products because it blocks melanin. It passes through the skin and has been tied to kidney and nerve damage. A global treaty bans products above 1 µg/g, yet they are still sold.

How sure: a systematic review of product tests and user samples worldwide. Read the review
