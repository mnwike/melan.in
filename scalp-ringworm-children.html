Scalp ringworm in children

Scalp ringworm is a common fungal infection in children, especially Black children. How to tell it from dandruff and why it needs prescribed treatment.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Scalp ringworm is a fungal infection, not a worm and not a sign of poor hygiene.
  • It is common in school-age children and especially common in Black children.
  • It needs treatment prescribed by a doctor. Oils, grease and shop creams will not clear it.

Strong evidence

What to look for

  • A scaly scalp that looks like stubborn dandruff.
  • Patches where hairs have snapped off, sometimes leaving black dots.
  • Round patches of hair loss.
  • Itching.
  • Swollen glands at the back of the neck.

Strong evidence

Why it matters to get it checked

The medical name is tinea capitis. It peaks between ages three and seven. In one dermatology clinic, Black patients made up 87% of all cases seen over 15 years. Scaling alone looks so much like dandruff that it is often missed, and doctors are advised to test any child with a scaly scalp. Treatment is a course of antifungal medicine taken by mouth.

Mixed evidence

Stopping it spreading

  • Do not share combs, brushes, hats or hair accessories.
  • Wash combs and brushes in hot soapy water.
  • Have brothers, sisters and others in the household checked.
  • Tell your child's barber or braider.

When to see a doctor

Any child with scalp scaling plus hair loss, broken hairs or swollen neck glands. A boggy, painful swelling on the scalp needs to be seen urgently.

Common questions

Is scalp ringworm the same as dandruff?

No. Ringworm is a fungal infection that can cause hair loss and needs prescribed treatment.

Can my child go to school with scalp ringworm?

Follow your doctor's advice. Children can usually attend once treatment has started.
