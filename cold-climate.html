Dark skin in a cold climate

What changes for melanin-rich skin and hair when you live far from the equator, and what the evidence supports doing about it.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Less sun means less vitamin D. UK health advice is for people with dark skin to consider a 10 microgram supplement every day, all year.
  • Cold, dry air and wind strip the skin's surface oils. Dryness shows as grey, "ashy" skin and needs richer moisturising than in warm, humid places.
  • Coiled hair dries out along its length. Winter studies are scarce, so advice here rests on what is known about the fibre.

Strong evidence

Vitamin D when the sun is low

Skin makes vitamin D from sunlight. Melanin filters that light, so darker skin needs longer in the sun to make the same amount. In the UK the sun is too low between October and early March for anyone to make vitamin D outdoors.

The UK government's nutrition advisers reviewed the evidence in 2016. Their advice for people with dark skin, including those of African, African-Caribbean and South Asian background, is to consider a daily 10 microgram supplement throughout the year. Babies under one need 8.5 to 10 micrograms a day, and children aged one to four need 10.

More is not better. The NHS publishes safe upper limits, and a pharmacist can check a dose against any medicines you take.

Sources: SACN, Vitamin D and health · UK government vitamin D guidance · NICE

Mixed evidence

Why skin turns dry and ashy

Cold temperatures, low humidity and wind deplete the lipid layer that keeps water in the skin. On darker skin the dry, flaking surface scatters light and looks grey.

Some small studies report that Black skin holds fewer ceramides, the fats that seal the skin's surface, and loses water more easily. A 2021 review led by Dr Andrew Alexis found these studies inconsistent and often poorly designed. The honest position is that the difference is plausible and not yet proven.

What helps

  • Gentle, lipid-rich cleansers in place of harsh soap. One small trial of a lipid-rich body wash reduced ashiness.
  • Thicker creams and ointments in winter, applied soon after washing.
  • Ingredients to look for: ceramides, glycerin, petrolatum, shea butter.

Sources: Alexis et al., skin barrier review · Talakoub and Wesley on ashiness

Strong evidence

Dark marks do not take the winter off

Any irritation, from eczema to a cracked patch of dry skin, can leave a dark mark on melanin-rich skin. These marks are among the most common reasons Black and brown patients see a dermatologist. Treating the cause early matters most, and sun protection is part of first-line care in every season.

Source: Davis and Callender, 2010

Limited evidence

Hair in winter

Tightly coiled hair is prone to dryness because scalp oils and products do not travel easily down the bends of the fibre. We found no good studies of afro-textured hair in cold weather specifically, so this section is expert guidance, not trial data.

  • Condition the lengths and ends. Piling product onto the scalp can cause build-up and flaking.
  • Keep braids and other protective styles low in tension.
  • Persistent itching, scaling or hair loss is a reason to see a doctor, not a dry-scalp problem to oil away.

Source: Dr Renée Beach, University of Toronto

Other supplements

Vitamin D is the only supplement on this page because it is the only one with official backing for this purpose. Others will be added one at a time, each with the trial that supports it.

When to see a doctor

Bone or muscle pain, skin that cracks or bleeds, a rash that does not settle in two weeks, or hair coming out in patches. Ask for a dermatologist with experience of darker skin if you can.
