How skin differs between populations

What research has measured about skin in people of African, European, East Asian and South Asian ancestry: melanin, barrier, ageing, scarring, and the limits.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Everyone has about the same number of pigment cells. The difference is how much melanin they make and how it is packaged.
  • Measured differences beyond colour are small, often disputed, and the groups overlap heavily.
  • What differs most reliably is how skin responds to sun and to injury.

Strong evidence

Read this first

The studies behind this page use labels such as 'African', 'Asian' and 'Caucasian'. Those are rough social groupings, not biological types. Each group contains huge variety, the ranges overlap, and many studies measured only a few dozen people in one city. Strong evidence

Pigment: the real difference

Melanin is made by melanocytes and handed to surrounding skin cells in packets called melanosomes. People of every ancestry have a similar number of melanocytes. In deeply pigmented skin the melanosomes are larger, more numerous, spread singly through the cell, and break down slowly, so pigment reaches the surface layers. In lightly pigmented skin they are smaller, clumped together, and degraded early. The type of melanin differs as well: more brown-black eumelanin in darker skin, relatively more yellow-red pheomelanin in fair and red-haired people.

Skin colour tracks the strength of ultraviolet light where a population's ancestors lived for a long time. Light skin evolved more than once, by different genes, in Europe and in East Asia. And the widest range of skin tones on Earth is found within Africa.

Mixed evidence

The outer barrier

The outermost layer has been reported to be equally thick in Black and white skin but built from more, tighter-packed cell layers in Black skin. Findings on water loss through the skin conflict: some studies find the least in Black skin, some the most, and a 2021 review concluded the differences probably reflect body site and small samples as much as biology. Ceramide levels, the fats that seal the barrier, have been reported highest in East Asian and lowest in Black skin, which is one proposed reason dryness and 'ashiness' are common complaints. The honest summary is that the data are thin and contradictory.

Limited evidence

The deeper layer

Reviews describe a thicker, more compact dermis in darker skin, with larger and more active fibroblasts, the cells that make collagen. Two consequences are suggested: wrinkles appear later, and scars are more likely to overgrow. Keloids are more common in people of African, and to a lesser degree Asian and Hispanic, ancestry.

Strong evidence

Response to sun

Deep pigmentation filters ultraviolet strongly. Sunburn, sun-driven wrinkling and sun-caused skin cancers are far less common. They are not absent. The trade-off is vitamin D: more melanin means less is made from the same sunlight, which matters at northern latitudes.

Strong evidence

Response to injury

The most consistent clinical difference: in darker skin of any ancestry, inflammation leaves pigment behind. A spot, graze, rash or burn is followed by a dark, or sometimes light, mark that can last months. This is why acne, eczema and shaving matter differently, and why lasers and peels need adjusting.

Mixed evidence

Ageing

In fair European skin, sun damage shows early as fine lines and broken veins. In East Asian skin it shows more as brown spots, with wrinkles later. In darker skin it shows later still, mostly as uneven tone and loss of volume in the mid-face.

Limited evidence

What is not known

Most research compared two or three groups in one country. South Asian, South-East Asian, Middle Eastern, Latin American, Pacific and mixed-heritage skin is barely studied. Diet, climate, skincare habits and income were rarely separated from ancestry.

When to see a doctor

None of these averages diagnoses anything. See a doctor about a symptom, not about a category.

Common questions

Do Black people have more melanocytes?

No. The number of pigment cells is similar in everyone. Darker skin makes more melanin and packages it in larger, longer-lasting packets.

Is Black skin thicker?

The outer layer is about the same thickness. Some studies report more, tighter-packed layers and a more compact deeper layer.
