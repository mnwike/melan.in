Hair loss from the crown (CCCA)

Central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia starts at the crown and spreads outward. Early signs, the PADI3 gene discovery, and why early treatment matters.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • CCCA is the most common scarring hair loss in women of African descent.
  • It starts at the crown and spreads outward. Lost follicles do not grow back.
  • Treatment aims to stop it spreading, so seeing a dermatologist early matters.

Strong evidence

What it is

CCCA stands for central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia. Inflammation around the follicles at the top of the scalp slowly replaces them with scar tissue. It usually appears in middle age. Many people notice tenderness, itching or hair breakage at the crown before any visible thinning.

Mixed evidence

What causes it

It was once called hot comb alopecia and blamed entirely on styling. In 2019 a team including Professor Ncoza Dlova and Dr Amy McMichael found mutations in a gene called PADI3, which helps form the hair shaft, in a share of affected women. The current view is that genes, family history and hair care practices all contribute.

Strong evidence

Treatment

A dermatologist can prescribe anti-inflammatory treatment to halt the process. Regrowth is not possible where follicles have scarred, which is why time matters. Bring a photo of your crown from a year or two ago if you have one.

When to see a doctor

Any thinning, tenderness or persistent itch at the crown. Ask whether a scalp biopsy is needed.

Common questions

Is CCCA caused by relaxers?

Not by relaxers alone. Genes play a part, and styling practices may add to the risk.

Can hair grow back after CCCA?

Not in areas that have scarred. Early treatment protects the hair you still have.
