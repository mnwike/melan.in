Dry and ashy skin

Why dry skin looks grey or ashy on darker skin tones, what the research says about the skin barrier, and how to moisturise so it lasts.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Ashiness is dry, flaking surface skin. It shows more on darker tones because the flakes look grey against the skin.
  • Cold, dry air and harsh soaps strip the oils that hold water in.
  • Richer cleansers and thicker moisturisers, applied after washing, are the fix.

Mixed evidence

Why it happens

The outer layer of skin is held together by fats, including ceramides. Cold temperatures, low humidity and wind deplete that layer and water escapes.

Some small studies report that Black skin has lower ceramide levels and loses water more easily. A 2021 review led by Dr Andrew Alexis found those studies inconsistent and often poorly designed. The difference is plausible and not proven.

Mixed evidence

What helps

  • Swap harsh soap for a gentle, lipid-rich cleanser. One small trial of a lipid-rich body wash reduced ashiness.
  • Moisturise soon after washing, while the skin is still slightly damp.
  • In winter, move from lotions to creams and ointments.
  • Look for ceramides, glycerin, petrolatum and shea butter on the label.

Strong evidence

When it is more than dryness

Dry patches that itch, thicken, or turn darker, greyer or purple can be eczema, which is more common and more often severe in people of African descent.

When to see a doctor

Skin that cracks or bleeds, itching that disturbs sleep, or dry patches that do not improve after two weeks of regular moisturising.

Common questions

Why does my skin look grey in winter?

Cold, dry air strips surface oils. The dry outer cells lift and flake, and on darker skin those flakes look grey or white.

Is shea butter enough on its own?

It seals water in but adds little. It works best over damp skin or over a cream that contains water-binding ingredients such as glycerin.
