Shea butter for skin: what it can and cannot do

Shea butter is a good moisturiser with a long history. What studies support, which claims lack evidence, and how to use it on dry or eczema-prone skin.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Shea butter is an emollient: it softens skin and slows water loss. That part is well accepted.
  • Early studies suggest it can ease eczema symptoms. Evidence for scars, stretch marks, acne and dark marks is lacking.
  • It works best over damp skin.

Strong evidence

What it is

Shea butter is fat pressed from the nuts of the African shea tree. It has been used on skin and hair across West Africa for generations and is now in countless creams.

Mixed evidence

What the evidence supports

Reference reviews rate it as a moisturiser and find early research that shea butter, alone or in a cream, improves eczema symptoms in children and teenagers. One 2015 study followed 34 people with eczema for four weeks using a cream that contained it. For acne, burns, scars, stretch marks and dandruff, reviewers found no good scientific evidence.

Limited evidence

How to use it

  • Apply to slightly damp skin after washing, or over a lighter lotion.
  • Use it on body skin, lips, hands and heels. On the face it can feel heavy.
  • Unrefined shea has a nutty smell and may contain more of the plant's natural compounds. Refined is odourless.

Limited evidence

Cautions

Shea butter does not lighten skin or fade marks, whatever a label says. Check the full ingredient list of any shea product sold as a brightening cream.

When to see a doctor

Dry skin that cracks, bleeds or itches enough to disturb sleep.

Common questions

Is shea butter good for dark skin?

It is a good moisturiser for dry skin of any tone. It does not change skin colour.

Does shea butter remove dark spots?

There is no good evidence that it does.
