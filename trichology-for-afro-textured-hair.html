Trichology for afro-textured hair

What a trichology assessment of afro-textured hair should cover: breakage, traction, CCCA, scalp flaking, relaxer and heat damage, and when it needs a doctor.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • Afro-textured hair has its own patterns of damage and its own hair loss conditions. The assessment should reflect that.
  • The commonest problems are breakage, traction at the hairline, and scarring loss at the crown.
  • Scarring conditions need a dermatologist early. A good trichologist will spot them and refer.

Mixed evidence

Why the hair type changes the consultation

Tightly coiled hair grows from a curved follicle and has a flattened, elliptical cross-section. Each bend is a point of lower strength, so it breaks more easily under combing and grooming than straighter hair. Natural scalp oil does not travel down a coil easily, so lengths run dry even when the scalp is oily. And the styles used to manage it, from braids to relaxers and heat, each carry a known pattern of damage. A practitioner who does not ask about styling history has missed half the examination.

Limited evidence

What should be checked

  • The hairline and temples for traction: thinning, short broken hairs, tiny bumps.
  • The crown for central thinning, tenderness, or a scalp that looks smooth and shiny.
  • The nape for breakage from collars, and in men for firm bumps.
  • The hair shaft for knots, splits and mid-shaft breaks, which show breakage and not loss from the root.
  • The scalp for scale, flaking, soreness and product build-up.
  • Your history: relaxers, heat, colour, braids, weaves, wigs, glue, wash frequency.
  • Health: iron, vitamin D, thyroid, recent illness, childbirth, medicines, family hair loss.

Mixed evidence

The conditions that matter most

  • Breakage. By far the commonest. Hair snaps along its length. The follicle is fine, so it recovers with gentler handling.
  • Traction alopecia. From repeated pull. Reversible early, permanent late.
  • CCCA. Scarring hair loss spreading out from the crown, the commonest scarring alopecia in women of African ancestry. Early signs are breakage and tenderness at the crown. Researchers have linked some cases to variants in a gene called PADI3, which helps form the hair shaft, so it is not simply 'caused by styling'.
  • Seborrhoeic dermatitis. Flaking and itch, often under-treated because washing is less frequent.
  • Acne keloidalis nuchae and razor bumps in men.
  • Scalp ringworm in children.
  • Frontal fibrosing alopecia and lichen planopilaris: scarring conditions that can be mistaken for traction.

Strong evidence

What the evidence base looks like

It is thin. A 2025 review searching for evidence-based care of textured hair in Black women found only 29 usable studies. Much of what is sold as hair science for afro hair has never been tested. That is a reason for humility from any practitioner, and for scepticism from you about confident claims.

Limited evidence

What good advice usually includes

  • Loosen, alternate and rest styles. Pain means too tight.
  • Wash the scalp regularly, more often if it flakes.
  • Detangle wet with conditioner, from the ends up.
  • Lower heat and space out chemical services.
  • Blood tests through a GP where the history suggests them.

When to see a doctor

Tenderness, burning or itching at the crown with thinning, any smooth shiny area, pustules or crusting, or a receding hairline that continues after styles are loosened.

Common questions

Should I see a trichologist or dermatologist for afro hair loss?

A trichologist is a good start for breakage and styling damage. Painful, shiny or scarring loss needs a dermatologist.

What is the most common hair problem in afro-textured hair?

Breakage. The hair snaps along its length while the follicle remains healthy.
