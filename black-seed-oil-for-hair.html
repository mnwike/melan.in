Black seed oil for hair and skin

Black seed oil, from Nigella sativa, is used for hair and skin across Africa, the Middle East and South Asia. What small studies show, and the allergy risk.

Not yet clinically reviewed · Last updated November 2026

The short answer

  • A few small studies suggest black seed oil may help some scalp and skin conditions. None is strong.
  • It is a reasonable conditioning oil. It is not a proven hair growth treatment.
  • Allergic skin reactions to it are well documented. Patch test.

Strong evidence

What it is

Black seed oil is pressed from the seeds of Nigella sativa, also called kalonji or black cumin. It has a long history in Islamic, Ayurvedic and African traditional medicine. Its main active compound is thymoquinone, which has anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects in the laboratory.

Limited evidence

What the evidence says

A handful of small trials report benefits for temporary hair shedding, eczema, acne and vitiligo when it is used in a lotion or cream. Each involved few people and short follow-up, and most have not been repeated. There is nothing on pattern baldness, traction alopecia or afro-textured hair.

Limited evidence

Using it

  • Dilute with a carrier oil for the scalp. It can sting neat.
  • Use a few times a week and wash out.
  • Stop at any itch, redness or blistering. Contact allergy is the main reported harm.
  • Swallowing it as a supplement is a separate matter with its own risks. Ask a pharmacist, especially in pregnancy or on medication.

When to see a doctor

Hair coming out in handfuls, bald patches, a sore or scaly scalp, or a rash after using an oil. No oil treats scarring hair loss, and delay costs hair.

Common questions

Is black seed oil good for hair?

It conditions hair, and small studies hint at scalp benefits, but the evidence is weak.
